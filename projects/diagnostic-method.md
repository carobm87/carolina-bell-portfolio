# Delivery and Risk Diagnostic Method

## Hypothetical worked examples

These are hypothetical business cases created for a senior PM interview panel. They are not client results. The point is to show the method I use to diagnose ambiguous problems, separate symptoms from causes, and make a decision under real constraints.

### Case 1: Fixed-price margin loss

A fixed-price engagement is losing margin even though the delivery team is working hard and individual projects appear manageable. The diagnosis is a structural estimation gap. The organization is treating repeated underestimation as a delivery problem when it is also a business decision about pricing, scope assumptions, and risk ownership.

The work focuses on identifying where the gap enters the system, making the pattern visible with data, and separating immediate delivery actions from the larger commercial decision.

### Case 2: Escalation and crisis management

An irreversible production-data incident has occurred. The immediate problem is operational, but the response also has to manage business impact, stakeholder trust, accountability, and the risk of creating a second failure through a rushed recovery.

The work focuses first on containment and shared facts, then on decision ownership, communication, recovery priorities, and the structural changes needed after the incident is stabilized.

## The five-step method

### 1. What is actually happening

Start with observable facts. Separate what is known from what is assumed. Define the current state, the affected users or teams, the business impact, and the constraints.

The goal is to stop the conversation from being driven by the loudest interpretation of the problem.

### 2. The real problem or pattern underneath

Look for the mechanism creating the visible symptom. Ask whether the issue is isolated or structural, and whether the organization is solving the right layer of the problem.

This is where repeated delivery pain can reveal a pricing problem, an ownership gap, a weak decision process, or a missing control.

### 3. The priority

Decide what must be handled first based on impact, reversibility, urgency, and risk. Not every important issue is the immediate priority.

The priority should be explicit enough that the team knows what not to optimize yet.

### 4. The actions

Define the smallest set of actions that changes the situation. Separate immediate containment from medium-term correction and longer-term structural work.

Actions need clear ownership, decision points, and evidence that shows whether the intervention is working.

### 5. The outcome

Define what success looks like in operational and business terms. The outcome should show that the original risk has been reduced, not simply that a set of tasks was completed.

Where possible, use measurable evidence so the organization can tell whether the pattern is actually changing.

## Signature moves

Three moves show up consistently in how I work:

- Separate the delivery problem from the business decision
- Make the invisible visible with data
- Frame fixes as protecting both the business and the team

This keeps the diagnosis practical. It also reduces the risk of solving a visible symptom while leaving the underlying decision problem untouched.
